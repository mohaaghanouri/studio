The best AI consultant sites in the EU and US

Eight parallel research agents visited roughly 200 unique sites across the US, DACH, UK/Ireland, continental Europe, the coaching and keynote circuits, the boutique-studio scene and the design-excellence tier. Every site was scored 1–10 on design, success, quality and expertise. These are the ten that survived.

How to read this

Scoring

Design — visual craft, typography, grid, colour restraint, motion, custom vs template, mobile. Success — named clients, case studies with numbers, real testimonials, books, stages, press, pricing transparency. Quality — copy clarity, information architecture, trust elements, conversion path, speed. Expertise — publications, technical writing, specific niche, AI substance versus buzzwords.

Agents were told to be harsh, to score against a global bar rather than a local one, to name template fingerprints where visible, and never to report a site they could not actually load. Where several agents scored the same site, the range is shown.

The only site in the whole sweep where copy, proof and conversion all land at once. The headline is an argument rather than a job title:

AI is making every company smarter. It isn't making any of them wiser.

The subhead then names the buyer's real fear — "Leaders don't need more information about emerging tech. They need better decisions they can defend." Take: the WAVE framework drawn as a diagram rather than asserted as a trademark, and the conversion trio of "Check Availability" + a 24-hour response promise + a downloadable brochure.

Across all 200 sites

What the sweep says

  1. Fame and site quality are almost uncorrelated. Bernard Marr (design 3–4), Sascha Lobo (4), Tom Goodwin (4) and David Shrier (1) are far more famous than anyone in the top 10. Sol Rashidi — the world's first enterprise CAIO, nine patents, Harvard Senior Fellow — has a hero that is a dense grey paragraph with fifteen inline blue links.
  2. Design is a wide-open gap in this category. In UK/Ireland, 14 of 24 sites run WordPress, Squarespace, HubSpot or Divi/Avada with default type, and only three had a deliberate font pairing. In DACH it is worse. A genuinely crafted site is a cheap and durable edge here in a way it would not be in, say, product design.
  3. Almost nobody publishes a number. Across ~200 sites, roughly a dozen publish prices. The ones that do — Paul Roetzer's $60,000 floor, WorkWise's 25-item menu, Crux Digits' four-rung ladder, Nicola Lazzari's three-currency day rates — stand out instantly. In DACH, where 20 of 24 hide behind "kostenloses Erstgespräch", a printed number is the cheapest differentiation available.
  4. A logo you can't explain is decoration. Parlance Labs and Prolego both show extraordinary logo walls (OpenAI, Stripe, Netflix; Lockheed, Citi, Morgan Stanley) with zero numbers or descriptions attached. One qualified sentence per logo would move either site two points.
  5. Fake proof is worse than no proof. Nicola Lazzari's site is beautifully built and publishes real metrics — then attributes its testimonials to "Sarah Chen", "David Kim", "Emma Rodriguez", with no companies. A stock-name set retroactively makes a skeptical buyer doubt the metrics too. An empty testimonials section scores higher than a fabricated one.
  6. The single highest-leverage asset is one defensible number about your own effect. Not a logo, not a testimonial, not a framework — a measured outcome. Conor Grennan's Microsoft RCT, Mark van Rijmenam's NPS 9.2, Jarno Duursma's per-event 9.4 and 9.6, Alice Labs' "$250K/year saved in six weeks". Every one of those beats a wall of Fortune 500 badges.
